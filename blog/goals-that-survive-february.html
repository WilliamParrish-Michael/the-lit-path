How to set goals that survive February

Every January, gyms fill up. Every February, they empty out. The popular explanation is willpower — people just didn't want it badly enough. That explanation is convenient, morally satisfying, and mostly wrong. Most goals don't fail in the doing. They fail in the design — weeks earlier, at the moment they were set, by a version of you that didn't have your calendar, your kids, or your Tuesday nights.

Design flaw #1: the goal is set for your best week

"Gym five days a week" works beautifully in a week with no sick kids, no deadline crunches, and no flat tires. You get maybe six of those weeks a year. A goal designed for your best week fails forty-six times a year by default, and every failure erodes the belief that you're a person who follows through.

The fix: design for your worst realistic week. Ask: what version of this could I do in the week everything goes sideways? Two sessions? One? That's the floor — the commitment. Anything above it is a bonus, not the standard. Floors build streaks; ceilings break them.

Design flaw #2: the goal describes an outcome you don't control

"Lose twenty pounds." "Get promoted." "Find a partner." These are results, and results are only partly yours to decide — biology, org charts, and other people all get a vote. Staking your sense of progress on a vote you don't control is how motivated people end up demoralized.

The fix: translate every outcome into behaviors you fully own. You can't control "get promoted"; you can control "have the career conversation with my manager this month" and "ship one visible project per quarter." Judge yourself only on the part that was actually yours.

Design flaw #3: nothing happens when you slip

Every plan meets a bad week eventually. Most goals have no answer for it, so the first slip becomes the quiet end — not because the miss was fatal, but because nothing was scheduled to catch it. The gap between "missed one week" and "abandoned in February" is usually just the absence of a scheduled restart.

The fix: decide the recovery rule in advance, when you're calm: a miss is followed by the smallest possible next rep, within 48 hours. Not a doubled make-up session — those are punishments, and people dodge punishments. Just the next small rep, quickly, so the streak of identity ("I'm someone who does this") survives even when the streak of execution doesn't.

Design flaw #4: nobody's expecting the update

Private goals are the easiest ones to quietly renegotiate. When the only person tracking the commitment is the person who'd rather skip it, the negotiation tends to go one way. This is the mundane, unglamorous reason coaching works: someone is expecting the update. Not to scold — to look at the miss with you and ask the useful question: what does this tell us about the design?

The February test

Before committing to any goal, run it through four questions: Could I do this in my worst realistic week? Is every part of it a behavior I control? Do I know exactly what happens after a slip? And does anyone besides me know the plan? Four yeses, and February is just another month.
