Five signs you're ready for a change — even if it doesn't feel like it

People imagine readiness as a feeling — a clean, confident moment where the fog lifts and the decision makes itself. In practice, that moment almost never comes. Readiness usually shows up in disguise, wearing clothes you'd never associate with progress: irritation, envy, boredom, repetition. Here are five of its most common costumes.

1. The same conversation keeps happening

You've described the problem to your partner, your best friend, your barber — and you notice you're using the same sentences every time. Word for word. When a story stops changing, it's because you've finished diagnosing and stopped short of deciding. The analysis is done. What's left is the part analysis can't do.

2. Other people's moves sting a little

A coworker leaves for something new. A friend finally opens the business. Someone from school posts about their move across the country. If your first flicker of feeling is a small, unwelcome ouch before the congratulations arrives — pay attention. Envy is terrible company but an excellent compass. You don't envy things you don't want.

3. "Someday" has developed suspicious precision

Someday-plans are supposed to be vague. Yours has details. You know which certification, which city, which conversation would have to happen first. You've priced it. A dream that's been quietly gathering specifics isn't a dream anymore — it's a plan waiting for a start date.

4. The comfortable thing has started to cost you

Nothing is wrong, exactly. The job is fine, the routine is fine, everything is fine — and you're tired in a way sleep doesn't fix. Staying put has a price tag too; it's just billed monthly, in small amounts, so it never triggers the alarm a big decision would. When "fine" starts feeling expensive, that's data.

5. You're reading this

Not a trick — a base rate. People who are genuinely settled don't spend their evenings reading about signs they're ready for a change. Some part of you queued this up on purpose. It's worth asking that part what it's trying to get you to admit.

None of these mean quit your job tomorrow

Recognizing readiness isn't the same as knowing what to do about it — that's a separate piece of work, and it goes better with structure and a second set of eyes. That's precisely the gap coaching fills: not deciding for you, but making sure the decision finally gets made, and then survives contact with real life.
