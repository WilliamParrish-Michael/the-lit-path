What a life coach actually does (and doesn't do)

Tell someone you're working with a life coach and you'll get one of two reactions: polite curiosity, or a barely hidden eyebrow. Fair enough — the term has been stretched over everything from serious professional practice to motivational posters. So let's be precise about what this work is, and just as important, what it isn't.

It isn't therapy

Therapy is healthcare. It treats conditions — anxiety, depression, trauma — and it looks backward as often as forward, because that's where healing often starts. Coaching does neither. A coach is not qualified to diagnose or treat anything, and a good one will say so plainly and refer you to a licensed professional when that's what the moment calls for.

Coaching starts from a different premise: you're not broken, you're stuck. The work faces forward. Where do you want to be, what's in the way, and what are you going to do about it this week?

It isn't advice

An advisor hands you their answer. A coach helps you find yours. That's not a stylistic preference — it's the whole mechanism. Advice you were given is easy to abandon the first hard week, because it was never really yours. A decision you reasoned your way to, out loud, with someone asking sharper and sharper questions? That one holds.

Expect a coach to ask things like: What would you do if you knew you couldn't fail? Fine — now what will you do given that you might? What are you pretending not to know? When you say you "don't have time," what are you actually choosing instead?

It definitely isn't a pep talk

Motivation is weather. It comes, it goes, and no serious plan should depend on it. A coach who only pumps you up is selling you a feeling that expires in the parking lot. The real work is structural: turning a vague ambition into a specific commitment, breaking the commitment into steps that fit an actual busy life, and then — this is the part people underestimate — coming back every week to check.

So what does the work look like?

  • Clarity first. Early sessions turn "something has to change" into a destination you can describe in one sentence.
  • Honest obstacles. Not just the practical ones. The stories you tell yourself about why now isn't the right time get examined too.
  • Commitments, not intentions. Every session ends with something specific you've agreed to do before the next one.
  • Accountability with teeth. When you show up not having done the thing, the interesting question isn't "why not?" — it's "what does that tell us about the plan?"

How to tell if it's the right tool

Coaching fits when you're functional but stuck: circling a decision, outgrowing a role, restarting after a big life change, or watching the same goal slide from one January to the next. It's the wrong tool when what's underneath is a health matter — that deserves a clinician, not a coach.

The honest test is a conversation. Which is why the first call is free, and why its only goal is to figure out — together — whether this is the right tool for where you are.
