When your loved one comes home from treatment

Families count the days until treatment ends. Then the day comes, and within a week many of them call us more frightened than they were before. That is normal, and it is not a sign that treatment failed. It is a sign that nobody warned them what the first weeks home actually look like.

What to expect

A newly sober person is raw. Thirty, sixty, ninety days is long enough to stop using and nowhere near long enough to learn how to live without it. Expect mood swings. Expect them to sleep too much or not at all. Expect them to be irritable with you specifically, because you are safe and the counselors are not around anymore.

Expect, also, that the house they are walking back into is full of triggers. The same room, the same drawer, the same friends texting. Treatment happened somewhere else. Home is where the old life lived, and the old life does not pack up and leave because they did.

What to watch for

Two things matter more than everything else. We tell every family about them before their loved one comes home.

  • Isolation. Skipping meetings. Not returning calls from the people they met in treatment. Spending more and more time in their room. Withdrawal is almost always the first thing that slips, and it slips quietly. If you notice them pulling away, say so, kindly, and early.
  • Suicidal behavior. Talk of hopelessness or being a burden. Giving away belongings. Saying goodbye in roundabout ways. Sudden calm after a long dark stretch. Take every one of these seriously, every time. Do not wait to see if it passes. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. For a crisis in the United States, call or text 988, any hour.

Other signs worth noticing: new secrecy about money or their phone, old friends reappearing, and a return of the small lies. None of these alone means relapse. All of them together mean it is time to call someone.

Holding boundaries without pushing them away

The boundaries you set before treatment still stand after it. A boundary is not a punishment. It is a statement about what you will do, for your own mental health and for everyone's physical safety. "I will not give you cash." "You cannot stay here if you are using." "I will not lie to your employer for you."

Say them calmly and keep them. The person who loves you and is trying will understand, even if they are angry in the moment. The person who is slipping will test them, and the test is the information you need.

You are not supposed to do this alone either

The family needs support as much as the person in recovery does. That is why we stay with families while treatment is underway and after it ends: for the questions, for the two a.m. fear, for the moment you are not sure whether what you are seeing is normal or a warning. Call us. If you are not sure whether it is worth a call, it is.
