What a sober companion actually does

Someone says yes to treatment. The family exhales. And then there is a gap: the hours, sometimes the day or two, between that yes and the moment they walk through the facility door. Ask anyone who has worked in this field where things fall apart, and they will tell you it is right there, in the gap.

A sober companion exists for the gap.

From decision to destination

The companion is there when the decision is made and does not leave until the person is checked in. That means the car ride. The airport. The flight, if the right facility is across the country, which it sometimes is. The layover. The hotel if there is one. The last cigarette in the parking lot. Every one of those is a place where "I just need to grab something first" turns into a disappearance, and the companion's whole job is to make sure it does not.

It is not a security job. Nobody is being restrained. The companion is a person who knows exactly what the drive to use feels like in those hours, because they felt it themselves, and who stays close enough that the person never has to face it alone.

Trained for the worst hours

Those hours carry real risk. Someone who has been using heavily can go into withdrawal on the way. Someone who slips and uses after a few days clean is at serious risk of overdose, because their tolerance has already dropped. Our companions are trained in overdose response and in recognizing relapse behavior as it starts, not after. They know what to watch for in a person's body and in their mood, and they know when to call for medical help.

If you ever face that moment without a companion present: call 911 immediately. Do not wait to see if it passes.

Keeping them in the world

The less obvious part of the job is social. A person on the way to treatment wants to go quiet. Headphones in, hood up, nobody talk to me. That silence is where the second thoughts grow. A good companion keeps them talking, keeps them eating, keeps them in the world. Not forced cheer. Just company, from someone who has sat in that exact seat.

Who does this work

Every companion we send has lived through addiction and come out the other side. That is not a nice-to-have. It is the whole point. When someone three hours from a facility says "I can't do this," the answer "yes you can, I did" only works if it is true.

What it looks like for the family

For the family, a sober companion means the day of departure is not yours to white-knuckle. You are not the one checking the bathroom door at the airport. You get to be the parent, the spouse, the sister, saying goodbye and meaning it, while someone else carries the job of getting them there.
